# Literature review: crypto market-structure prediction

## Scope and decision standard

This review asks whether information available at forecast time can predict **risk and market structure**—future volatility, drawdown risk, dispersion, correlation, breadth, and opportunity—more reliably than it predicts returns. It is a scoping study, not evidence that any feature is deployable. A result is decision-relevant only if it beats a leakage-free price-only baseline in rolling out-of-sample tests after realistic data lags, revisions, missingness, and costs.

The strongest prior is that volatility is persistent and therefore forecastable. The harder question is whether alternative data adds stable information after lagged volatility, volume, liquidity, and broad-market state are included. The literature gives plausible mechanisms, but relatively few studies perform source-timestamped, cross-provider, untouched-holdout tests. Publication-year coverage is through 22 June 2026; peer-reviewed 2026 evidence is still sparse.

## Findings by requested theme

1. **Volatility forecasting:** the evidence is strongest here. Lagged realized volatility, intraday information, volume, and liquidity create a hard baseline. ML sometimes improves forecast loss, especially at short horizons, but external features do not consistently dominate internal market history.
2. **Drawdown prediction:** economically valuable but statistically difficult. Crashes are rare, labels overlap, and threshold choice can manufacture results. A calibrated drawdown probability is preferable to a hard classifier.
3. **Regime prediction:** HMM and network approaches describe state changes well. Their predictive value must be assessed using state estimates generated only from past data.
4. **Dispersion forecasting:** direct prospective literature is thinner than volatility research. Herding and connectedness studies support the mechanism; they do not yet establish a stable production forecast.
5. **Correlation forecasting:** dynamic-network results show meaningful time variation. Estimation noise and universe turnover are central problems.
6. **Cross-sectional opportunity forecasting:** opportunity is a composite decision target, not a natural observable. It can be useful operationally but is vulnerable to researcher degrees of freedom.
7. **On-chain asset pricing:** adoption, activity and attention contain information, but provider transformations, chain differences, revisions and publication lags can erase apparent gains.
8. **Derivatives forecasting:** funding, basis, open interest, options implied volatility/skew, and liquidations have clear leverage and risk-transfer mechanisms. This is the most promising incremental feature family for volatility and drawdown risk.
9. **Stablecoin flows:** issuance is not automatically buying pressure. Mint/burn, treasury, bridge, exchange, peg and stablecoin design states must be distinguished.
10. **Exchange flows:** exchange deposits can proxy sell-side inventory and stress, but entity labeling and internal wallet reshuffles are material confounders.
11. **Market microstructure:** adverse selection, spread, depth, imbalance, fragmentation and price discovery are plausible short-horizon predictors. Historical full-depth data and venue normalization are the practical bottlenecks.
12. **ML for risk:** calibration, tail metrics, stability, and economic decisions matter more than generic classification accuracy.
13. **Deep learning:** useful when sample size, panel breadth and mixed-frequency inputs justify it. In a daily crypto study, boosted trees and regularized linear models are stronger first-line choices.
14. **2023–2026 literature:** newer studies add graph methods, tuned neural volatility models, on-chain factors and regime frameworks, but still rarely combine point-in-time alternative data with an untouched holdout. There is not yet persuasive evidence that a foundation model is the default for this problem.

## Research gaps that matter

- Point-in-time vendor timestamps and metric revisions are usually under-specified.
- Survivorship-free asset membership and delisted-market history are rarely handled convincingly.
- “Alternative data helps” is often tested against a weak benchmark rather than HAR-RV plus price, volume, liquidity and market-state features.
- Hyperparameter and target searches are seldom charged against statistical significance.
- Forecast-loss improvement is not always translated into a decision such as volatility targeting, exposure control, option hedging, or risk budgeting.
- Results based on one asset, venue, provider, or market regime have limited deployment value.

## Practical conclusion

The literature supports a staged study of **future realized volatility / volatility expansion**, using derivatives and market-microstructure data as incremental feature blocks. It does not support jumping directly to a deep model, a composite opportunity target, or a return strategy. Drawdown and correlation-regime forecasts are worthwhile secondary tasks once the volatility pipeline is point-in-time and provider-robust.
